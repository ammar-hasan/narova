# Scene direction

Read this only when spatial relationships, appearance, staging, camera,
interaction, or compositing are material risks. Use the questions that change
the work; skip the rest.

## Direct relationships, not inventory

Decide what the viewer finds first and what becomes clear later. Arrange the
accepted subjects and world around the separation, enclosure, distance, scale,
alignment, transformation, or reveal that carries the idea. Use foreground,
subject plane, background, negative space, and atmosphere deliberately. Give
important forms support, clearance, depth, and a recognizable scale anchor when
scale matters.

Materials and light should explain identity, focus, and spatial change. Choose
surface response, texture scale, contrast, shadow, reflection, environment,
fog, or atmosphere only where they help. There is no required key/fill/rim
setup, realism level, object density, or finish. Judge the 3D, typography,
captions, generated media, and effects as one image.

## Give the camera a perceptual job

For each meaningful move, identify its subject, what it reveals or makes felt,
and where attention should finish. Then choose any framing, projection, lens
character, path, orientation, and tempo that serves that job.

Reason in the actual space:

- keep the intended target visible when it must be understood;
- use occlusion deliberately for discovery, suspense, scale, or transition;
- preserve clearance from solid geometry unless crossing it is intentional;
- check start, end, closest approach, turns, and occlusion changes; and
- preserve position, screen direction, scale, and attention when continuity
  matters.

An orbit, dolly, crane, cut, locked frame, handheld path, or impossible graphic
camera can all be right. Smooth movement without a perceptual job is not
direction.

## Stage interaction as visible state change

Write the smallest causal account before animating material interaction:

```text
initial state -> cause -> contact or constraint -> response -> changed state
```

Let the viewer see the relevant before, event, and after. At decisive instants,
compare position, orientation, speed, deformation, attachment, support,
clearance, and timing as applicable. Authored motion may be better than
simulation for a controlled deterministic event; simulation may be appropriate
when emergent contact is essential and inspectable. Never call keyframed
plausibility verified simulation.

Watch for interpenetration, hovering, tunneling, response before contact,
unmotivated constraint changes, or camera motion hiding the causal instant. A
cut, sound, label, or replay may support an event but cannot substitute for a
missing contact or contradictory state. Simplify, change representation, or
stop when the event cannot be made coherent.
